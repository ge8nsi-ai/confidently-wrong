# Immune memory and vaccines

A vaccine does not put a small amount of illness into the body and let it be
fought off. It presents the immune system with something that looks like a
pathogen — an inactivated virus, a fragment of surface protein, or the mRNA
instructions for building that fragment — without the machinery the real pathogen
uses to replicate and cause disease.

The response happens in two waves. The innate system reacts within hours to
anything that looks foreign, and it does not distinguish between one pathogen and
another. The adaptive system takes days and is specific: B cells that happen to
carry a receptor matching the antigen are selected, multiply, and mature into
plasma cells that secrete antibodies. T cells do a parallel job, killing infected
cells and coordinating the response.

The point of vaccination is the residue left behind. A fraction of those selected
B and T cells persist as memory cells, sometimes for decades. On a later
encounter with the real pathogen, the adaptive response no longer has to search
for a matching receptor from scratch; the matching cells are already present in
large numbers and respond in hours rather than days. The infection is usually
cleared before it produces symptoms.

Two consequences follow that people often get wrong. First, protection is not
binary. A vaccinated person can still be infected, and can still transmit; what
falls sharply is the probability of severe disease, because the pathogen is
contained early. Second, a booster is not topping up a tank of antibodies.
Circulating antibody levels do decline over months, but the booster works by
re-stimulating memory cells, which both raises antibody levels again and improves
the average fit between antibody and antigen through further maturation.

Some vaccines need adjuvants — substances that provoke the innate system enough
that it takes the antigen seriously. A purified protein injected on its own is
often ignored. This is why the formulation of a vaccine matters as much as the
choice of antigen, and why an aluminium salt appears in vaccines that have nothing
else in common.
