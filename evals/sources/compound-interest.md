# Compound interest

Interest is compound when the interest already earned starts earning interest
itself. Simple interest on 1,000 at 10 per cent pays 100 a year forever. Compound
interest pays 100 in the first year, then 110 in the second, because the second
year's interest is calculated on 1,100 rather than on the original 1,000.

The growth this produces is exponential, not linear, and people badly
underestimate it over long horizons. A common instinct is to guess that 1,000
growing at 7 per cent for 30 years ends up somewhere near 3,000 — the 30 years of
70 a year that simple interest would give, plus a bit. The actual figure is about
7,612. The intuition fails because each year's gain is larger than the last, and
the differences accumulate on top of one another.

A useful shortcut is the rule of 72: divide 72 by the annual percentage rate to
get the approximate number of years for money to double. At 6 per cent, doubling
takes about 12 years. At 9 per cent, about 8. The rule is an approximation that
works best between roughly 5 and 12 per cent.

Compounding frequency matters, but far less than the rate does. The same 12 per
cent nominal rate compounded monthly rather than annually gives an effective
annual rate of 12.68 per cent. Compounded daily it reaches 12.75 per cent. Pushed
to continuous compounding it converges on 12.75 per cent and no higher; the limit
is e raised to the rate, so there is no unbounded gain from compounding more
often. This is why the advertised APR and the effective annual rate can differ,
and why the effective rate is the one to compare on.

The same mechanism runs in reverse on debt. A credit card at 24 per cent nominal,
compounded monthly, costs an effective 26.8 per cent a year. Paying only the
minimum leaves most of the balance in place, so the interest is charged on nearly
the same principal each month, and a balance that feels small can take years to
clear. Inflation compounds in the same way, which is why 3 per cent a year halves
purchasing power in a little over 23 years.
