# Tides

The Moon raises two tidal bulges on Earth, not one. This surprises people who
picture the Moon simply lifting the water beneath it, and the second bulge — on
the far side of the planet, where the Moon is furthest away — is the part that
gives the mechanism away.

Tides are caused by the *difference* in the Moon's gravitational pull across the
width of the Earth, not by the pull itself. The near side of the Earth is about
12,700 kilometres closer to the Moon than the far side, and gravity weakens with
distance. The ocean on the near side is therefore pulled slightly harder than the
solid Earth as a whole, and the ocean on the far side slightly less hard. Relative
to the centre of the planet, water at both ends is left behind — one lot pulled
ahead, the other lot lagging. That is why there are two bulges, and why most
coastlines see two high tides a day rather than one.

Because the Earth rotates through both bulges, a given coast passes a high tide
roughly every 12 hours and 25 minutes. The extra 25 minutes exist because the
Moon has moved along its orbit in the meantime, so the Earth must turn a little
further to catch up with it.

The Sun contributes tides too, but less than the Moon does, despite being vastly
more massive. Tidal force falls off with the cube of distance rather than the
square, and the Sun is roughly 390 times further away. Its tidal effect works out
at about 45 per cent of the Moon's. When the Sun and Moon line up, at new and full
moon, their bulges add and the tides are unusually large — spring tides, a name
that refers to the water springing up rather than to the season. When they sit at
right angles, at the quarter moons, the bulges partly cancel and the range is
small: neap tides.

Local geography matters more than any of this for the height actually observed. A
funnel-shaped bay such as the Bay of Fundy concentrates the incoming water into a
range of over 15 metres, while parts of the Mediterranean barely register a tide
at all. The open-ocean tidal bulge itself is under a metre high.
